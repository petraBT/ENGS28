    Subsection 1.4.3 Part 3: External LEDs on the Breadboard

    The on-board LED is convenient for software testing, but the whole point of a microcontroller is to control external devices. You will wire an LED on a breadboard, drive it from a GPIO pin, and use a digital multimeter to measure the voltages and currents in the circuit.
    🔗
    Your breadboard has power rails running along each long edge. Some kits come with these rails already wired to the Nucleo; if yours does not, wire them now as shown in Figure 1.4.4. Use the Nucleo’s 3.3 V header pin for the positive rails. Also connect all positive rails to each other and all ground rails to each other as shown — this lets you tap 3.3 V or GND from anywhere on the board. Once wired, leave all of these jumpers in place for the entire term.
    🔗
    To build the LED circuit, choose a red or green LED and a 220 Ω resistor. Wire the circuit as shown in Figure 1.4.5: the GPIO pin connects to the LED anode (+, longer lead), the LED cathode (−, shorter lead) connects through the resistor to the ground rail.
    🔗
    The digital multimeter (Figure 1.4.6) is essential for verifying circuits. Set the dial to the 20 V DC range for 3.3 V circuits; turn it off when finished to preserve the battery.
    🔗

    Activity 1.4.1. Wire and Measure.

    Update your blinky program to use delay_ms(2000) (2 s on, 2 s off) so the DMM display has time to settle.
    🔗

    (a)

    Flash your updated program. Confirm that both the on-board LED and the external LED blink.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    With the LED lit, measure three voltages. \(V_{CC}\text{:}\) from the power rail to ground (expect ≈ 3.3 V). \(V_F\text{:}\) across the LED (anode to cathode). \(V_R\text{:}\) across the resistor. Verify Kirchhoff’s voltage law: \(V_{CC} = V_F + V_R\text{.}\) Calculate the current \(I_F = V_R / R\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    Replace the 220 Ω resistor with a 1 kΩ resistor. Repeat the measurements. Calculate the new current. Is the LED brighter or dimmer? Why?
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Activity 1.4.2. Move to a New Pin.

    Move the LED’s anode wire from D13 to D4 (Figure 1.4.7). To make the LED blink from D4 you need to make three changes to your program: enable the correct GPIO port clock, configure the pin as an output, and drive it. Before writing any code, answer the following on paper.
    🔗

    (a)

    Using Figure 1.4.8, find which STM32 port and bit number D4 corresponds to.
    🔗
    🔗

    (b)

    In the STM32C031C6 Reference Manual, go to Section 5.4 (RCC registers) and find the RCC_IOPENR register description. Which bit enables the clock for the port you identified?
    🔗
    🔗

    (c)

    From Day 1: for pin \(n\text{,}\) MODER uses bits \(2n\) and \(2n+1\text{.}\) What are the two MODER bit numbers for the pin you identified? What value encodes output mode?
    🔗
    🔗

    (d)

    Update your code with the values you found and verify the external LED blinks. The on-board LED (PA5) will stop blinking — that is expected.
